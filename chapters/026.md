## Parable 6 — The Two Shepherds

I was sitting at home, thanking the Lord for everything I'd been shown. My thoughts kept returning to the commandments. They were beautiful and powerful, full of joy and glory, and they could save a person's soul.

*I'll be blessed if I walk in them,* I thought. *Anyone who walks in them will be blessed.*

Suddenly the shepherd was sitting beside me.

“Why are you still doubting the commandments I gave you? They are beautiful. Don't doubt them. Clothe yourself in faith in the Lord, and you will walk in them. I will strengthen you.”

He reminded me that repentance without obedience was empty. Those who repented had to throw off the evil practices of this world—the things that crushed them—and clothe themselves instead with righteousness. If they stopped adding new sins to the old ones and walked in the commandments, they would live to God.

Then he stood.

“Let's go into the country. I want to show you the shepherds of the sheep.”

We came to a broad plain where I saw a young shepherd wearing a light, saffron-colored cloak. A great flock surrounded him. The sheep were well fed and energetic, some of them leaping and running everywhere.

The young shepherd seemed just as delighted as they were. He moved among them with a bright face, sharing their excitement.

“Do you see this shepherd?” my guide asked.

“Yes, Sir.”

“He is the angel of self-indulgence and deceit.”

I hadn't expected that.

“He destroys the souls of God's servants by turning them away from the truth. He leads them into evil desires until they forget the commandments of the living God. Some he leads to death, and others to corruption.”

“What do you mean by *death* and *corruption*?”

He pointed toward the sheep. Those that were joyfully leaping about, he explained, represented people who had completely separated themselves from God and surrendered themselves to the desires of this world. Through them God's name was blasphemed. Their life had become death.

But there were other sheep. They were well fed too, but they stayed in one place instead of leaping around.

These also represented people who had given themselves to indulgence and deceit, but they had not blasphemed the Lord. They had wandered from the truth, yet repentance was still open to them.

“Corruption still has the hope of renewal,” he said. “Death ends in destruction.”

We walked a little farther.

Then I saw another shepherd.

He was enormous and wild-looking, with a white goatskin thrown over him and a bag hanging from his shoulder. In one hand he carried a hard, knotted staff. In the other was a large whip.

His face terrified me.

The young shepherd began handing sheep over to him—the well-fed ones that had indulged themselves but had not been leaping about. The severe shepherd drove them into a steep place covered with thorns and briars. They became tangled there, unable to free themselves.

Then he beat them.

He drove them back and forth through the thorns and gave them no rest. Watching it made me miserable.

“Sir, who is this shepherd? He's cruel and severe. He doesn't seem to have any compassion for these sheep.”

“He is the angel of punishment,” my guide said. “He is one of the righteous angels.”

Those who wandered from God into the desires and deceptions of this life were handed over to him, and he punished them according to what they had done.

“What kinds of punishments?”

“The sufferings of this present life.”

He named losses, poverty, illnesses, constant instability, humiliation at the hands of unworthy people, and many other kinds of suffering. Some people watched one plan after another collapse. They threw themselves into new efforts, but nothing seemed to succeed.

And instead of examining themselves, they blamed God.

Yet the shepherd told me that these afflictions could become instruction. Through suffering, some were strengthened again in faith. They remembered the evil they had done and finally understood why they were suffering.

Then they praised God as a just judge.

They returned to him and served him with pure hearts for the rest of their lives. Their lives became fruitful again, and they thanked God even for having been handed over to punishment.

I had another question.

“Do people suffer under punishment for the same amount of time that they lived in self-indulgence?”

“Yes.”

That didn't seem severe enough to me.

“Then their punishment is very small. Someone who forgets God and lives in self-indulgence should suffer seven times as long.”

“You're foolish,” he said. “You don't understand the power of the punishment.”

“That's true. If I understood it, I wouldn't be asking.”

He explained that the two kinds of time weren't equal. An hour of self-indulgence was brief, but an hour of torment carried the weight of thirty days. A day surrendered to indulgence could therefore bring suffering with the force of a year.

The pleasure was short.

The consequences were long.

I still wasn't getting it.

“Sir, I don't completely understand.”

“Your stupidity stays with you,” he said. “You refuse to clear your heart and serve God. Be careful that the time doesn't run out while you're still trapped in your foolishness.”

Then, because I kept asking, he explained again.

Self-indulgence has a short memory. A person gives himself whatever he wants today and tomorrow barely remembers what he did. Pleasure passes quickly, leaving foolishness behind.

Suffering remembers.

When punishment remains with someone, day after day, he eventually remembers the indulgence that brought him there. What seemed insignificant when he chose it becomes clear through the long consequences that follow.

“They possessed life,” the shepherd said, “but handed themselves over to death.”

“What kinds of self-indulgence are harmful?”

His answer was broader than I expected.

Anything could become self-indulgence when someone surrendered himself to the pleasure of his own passion. An angry man indulged himself when he let anger run freely. So did the adulterer, the drunkard, the slanderer, the liar, the greedy person, and the fraud.

Each person had his own passion.

Each could loosen the reins and let it carry him wherever it wanted.

These indulgences harmed the servants of God and brought suffering upon them. If people continued in them without repentance, they eventually brought death upon themselves.

But then the shepherd surprised me.

There was another kind of self-indulgence.

Some people became almost carried away by the pleasure of doing good. They delighted in goodness so much that they gave themselves freely to it.

That kind of pleasure was worth surrendering to.

It was good for the servants of God.

And instead of leading them toward punishment and death, it led them into life.
