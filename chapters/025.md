## Parable 5 — The Fast God Desires

Early one morning I was sitting on a mountain, fasting and thanking the Lord for everything he had done for me, when the shepherd appeared and sat beside me.

“Why have you come here so early?”

“I'm keeping a fast, Sir.”

“What kind of fast?”

I wasn't sure what he meant. “The kind I usually keep.”

“You don't know how to fast to the Lord,” he said. “What you're doing isn't really a fast at all.”

That caught me off guard.

“Why do you say that?”

“Because the fast you think you're offering God accomplishes nothing for righteousness. I'll show you the complete fast, the one that is acceptable to him.”

He told me to begin somewhere deeper than food. Do no evil. Serve the Lord with a pure heart. Keep his commandments, walk in his ways, and don't allow evil desires to take root within you. Trust God, fear him, and turn away from every evil deed.

“Do these things,” he said, “and you will accomplish a great fast, one that is pleasing to God.”

Then he gave me a parable.

A man owned a large estate with many servants. Part of his land was planted as a vineyard, and before leaving on a journey, he called one of his most trusted servants.

“Fence this vineyard while I'm gone. Do nothing else to it. Keep this command, and you will have your freedom in my house.”

The servant did exactly what he had been told. He built the fence. But when he finished, he looked over the vineyard and noticed weeds everywhere.

He thought to himself, *I've done what my master commanded. But if I dig the soil and remove these weeds, the vineyard will be healthier. The vines won't be choked, and they'll produce more fruit.*

So he went back to work.

He dug the vineyard and pulled out every weed. By the time he finished, the place was clean and flourishing.

When the master returned and saw what had happened, he was delighted. The servant had obeyed his command, but he hadn't stopped there. He had seen another good thing that needed doing and had done it willingly.

The master called his beloved son, his heir, along with his trusted advisers.

“I promised this servant freedom if he obeyed me,” he told them. “But he not only obeyed; he did something good beyond what I commanded. So I want to make him a fellow heir with my son.”

The son agreed.

A few days later, the master held a feast and sent the servant a generous portion of food. The servant kept only what he needed and distributed the rest among his fellow servants. They rejoiced at his generosity and prayed that the master would show him even greater favor.

When the master heard about this too, he rejoiced all over again.

By then I knew everything in the story had to mean something.

Unfortunately, I had no idea what.

“Sir, I don't understand these parables unless you explain them to me.”

He told me first to keep God's commandments. That alone was pleasing to God. But when someone obeyed what God had commanded and then freely added other good works, there was even greater glory in it.

“What you've commanded me, I'll do,” I told him. “I know you're with me.”

“I am with you because you're eager to do good,” he said. “And I will be with everyone who has that same desire.”

Then he returned to my fast.

Fasting was good, he said, when it rested upon obedience. First I was to keep myself from evil words and evil desires and clear my heart of the empty things of this world. Then, on the day I fasted, I was to eat only bread and water.

But the fast wasn't supposed to end with my own hunger.

I was to calculate what I would normally have spent on food that day and give that amount to a widow, an orphan, or someone in need. My own self-denial would then become another person's provision, and the one who received it could eat and pray to the Lord for me.

“That sacrifice is acceptable to God,” he said. “It is beautiful, joyful, and pleasing to the Lord.”

He told me to practice this with my household and teach it to others.

But I still hadn't forgotten the vineyard.

“Please explain the rest of the parable,” I said. I started listing everything—the estate, the master, the vineyard, the servant, the fence, the weeds, the son, the advisers.

He was not impressed.

“You are unbelievably persistent with your questions. You don't need to ask about everything. If something should be explained to you, it will be explained.”

“But Sir, if you show me something and don't explain it, then I've seen it for nothing. And if you tell me a parable without explaining it, I've heard it for nothing.”

He reminded me that anyone who truly has the Lord in his heart can ask God for understanding. God is generous with those who keep asking him.

But I had an answer for that too.

“Sir, I have you here. Why wouldn't I ask you? You're the one showing me these things. If I'd seen them without you, then I would have asked the Lord.”

He gave in.

“I already told you that you're shamelessly persistent. But since you're so stubborn, I'll explain it so you can teach it to everyone else.”

The estate, he said, was the world. The master was God, who created everything and ordered it. The servant was the Son of God, and the vines were the people God had planted. The fences were the holy angels who guarded God's people, while the weeds were their sins.

The food sent from the feast represented the commandments given to the people through the Son. The advisers were the holy angels created first, and the master's journey represented the time remaining until his coming.

I was amazed.

“Sir, these things are incredible. How could I possibly have understood all that on my own?”

“You couldn't,” he said. “No one could, no matter how understanding he was.”

Naturally, I had another question.

“Why, then, is the Son of God shown as a servant?”

“You still don't understand,” he said. “The Son isn't being shown as powerless. He's shown with great authority.”

God planted the vineyard—he created the people—and entrusted them to his Son. The Son placed angels over them and himself cleansed away their sins through great labor and suffering. He showed them the paths of life and gave them the law he had received from his Father.

“He is Lord of the people,” the shepherd said. “He has received all authority from his Father.”

Then he explained the part about the inheritance.

God caused the holy pre-existent Spirit, through which creation came to be, to dwell in the flesh he had chosen. That flesh submitted completely to the Spirit. It lived in holiness and purity without defiling the Spirit dwelling within it.

It labored with the Spirit, cooperating faithfully, courageously, and without corruption. Because it had served the Spirit without blame, God chose that flesh to share with the Holy Spirit and receive the reward of its faithful service.

“All flesh in which the Holy Spirit dwells, if it remains pure and undefiled, will receive a reward.”

At last, he said, I had the interpretation.

And I was glad to have it.

Then he brought the teaching back to me.

“Keep your own flesh pure and undefiled so that the Spirit dwelling within you can testify on its behalf. Never tell yourself that your flesh doesn't matter because it will perish anyway. Don't use that as permission to defile it. If you defile the flesh, you defile the Holy Spirit as well.”

I immediately thought about the past.

“But Sir, what about someone who did these things in ignorance before hearing this teaching? How can someone who has already defiled his flesh be saved?”

“God alone has power to heal the things done in former ignorance. All authority belongs to him.”

There was mercy in that answer, but there was also responsibility.

From this point forward, I was to keep myself pure. The Lord Almighty was full of compassion and could heal what had been done before, but I was not to return to defilement.

The flesh and the Spirit shared this life together. What happened to one could not simply be separated from the other.

“Keep them both pure,” he told me, “and you will live to God.”
