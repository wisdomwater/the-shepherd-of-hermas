## Preface

I had heard of *The Shepherd of Hermas* long before I actually read it. The name would occasionally appear in conversations about the early church, usually alongside other writings that didn't make it into the New Testament. I knew it had been important. I knew some early Christians had regarded it very highly. Beyond that, though, I couldn't have told you much about what was actually in it.

There is something strange about realizing that a book could matter deeply to Christians living only a few generations after the apostles and yet be almost completely unknown to many Christians today. I grew up in a Christian world where the Bible was at the center of everything, and most of my reading naturally stayed within those boundaries. We might occasionally hear the names of the Church Fathers or learn something about the councils of the early church, but there was an enormous stretch of Christian history between the last page of the New Testament and the Christianity I knew. I had rarely wandered very far into it.

So I came to *The Shepherd of Hermas* mostly out of curiosity. I wanted to hear one of those early voices for myself.

I wasn't prepared for how strange that voice would be.

Hermas doesn't give us a theological treatise. He gives us visions. An elderly woman appears and speaks to him, then changes in appearance as the visions continue. A tower rises upon the waters, built from stones that represent human lives. Angels build. Virgins carry stones. Trees become symbols. Mountains are filled with meaning. An enormous beast approaches Hermas on the road. Eventually a shepherd arrives and begins giving him commandments and explaining parables, while Hermas does what Hermas seems to do best: asks questions.

A lot of questions.

That was one of the things that gradually endeared him to me. Hermas is not a polished spiritual master calmly explaining the mysteries of God to the rest of us. He is the one receiving them, and quite often he has no idea what is going on. He sees something extraordinary and tries to understand it. He guesses wrong. He asks for an explanation. Sometimes he receives one and immediately asks another question. His heavenly guides occasionally become impatient with him, and he can be surprisingly persistent in return.

There is something wonderfully human about him.

As I spent more time with the work, however, the strangeness became more than strange imagery. I began encountering a form of Christianity that felt both familiar and unfamiliar at the same time. Hermas speaks constantly about repentance, purity, truthfulness, generosity, self-control, marriage, wealth, desire, suffering, and care for people in need. He is deeply concerned with how Christians actually live and with what kind of people they are becoming.

Some of his teachings may feel immediately recognizable. Others may not. Some may feel beautiful. Some may feel severe. There are places where a modern reader may find Hermas encouraging and others where we may find ourselves uncomfortable with what he says about repentance, judgment, obedience, or the consequences of failure.

I think that discomfort is worth allowing.

One temptation when reading an ancient Christian work is to make it agree with us too quickly. We can translate unfamiliar ideas into categories we already understand, soften what troubles us, emphasize what confirms our theology, and quietly explain away whatever doesn't. Before long, the ancient author begins sounding remarkably like someone from our own church, denomination, or theological tradition.

I didn't want to do that with Hermas.

The purpose of this retelling is not to turn *The Shepherd of Hermas* into a twenty-first-century Christian book. It is to let an ancient Christian book speak in twenty-first-century language. Those are very different things.

I have therefore tried to remove one kind of distance while preserving another. The language should feel natural. Hermas should sound like a person telling you what happened rather than like someone speaking through the grammar of an old translation. When he doesn't understand something, he can simply say that he doesn't understand. When he is frightened, confused, curious, or hopeful, I have tried to let us stand there with him rather than explain his experience from a distance.

But the world he inhabits remains his world.

The tower is still a tower. The virgins remain virgins. Angels appear and disappear. Stones are accepted, rejected, shaped, and returned. Repentance is sometimes spoken of in ways that may not fit neatly within our own theology. The warnings remain warnings. I have not tried to rescue Hermas from his strangeness, because increasingly I came to believe that the strangeness is part of the reason to read him.

There is also no requirement that we agree with him.

That may be one of the most important invitations I can offer before we begin. Reading an early Christian text does not require us to pretend that every early Christian believed exactly the same things, nor does historical importance make every statement authoritative. *The Shepherd of Hermas* was treasured by many early Christians and came remarkably close to the edges of what some communities regarded as Scripture. It was copied alongside biblical books and read as valuable spiritual instruction, but it ultimately remained outside the New Testament canon.

Perhaps that gives us a particularly interesting way to approach it. We don't have to come to Hermas looking for doctrines we are obligated to defend. We can simply listen.

We can ask what kind of Christianity produced a book like this. We can notice what worried these Christians and what gave them hope. We can watch how they thought about failure and repentance, wealth and poverty, temptation and virtue, community and responsibility. We can allow ourselves to be surprised by the places where their concerns overlap with ours and equally surprised by the places where they don't.

Most of all, we can walk beside Hermas.

That is what I have tried to do in these pages. I have imagined him not as a distant figure carved into the history of Christianity, but as an ordinary person trying to understand extraordinary experiences. He sees. He wonders. He misunderstands. He asks. He is corrected. He learns. And, slowly, he changes.

There are moments when I wanted his heavenly guides to be gentler with him. There are moments when I wanted Hermas to understand something sooner than he did. There are teachings I find compelling and others I continue to wrestle with. But perhaps that is what happens when we genuinely listen to another voice instead of merely searching it for echoes of our own.

Nearly two thousand years separate us from the community that first heard these visions. Their world is not ours, and their questions are not always our questions. Yet beneath all that distance, something surprisingly recognizable remains: a human being wondering what it means to live well before God, what to do after we have failed, how we become different from who we have been, and whether there is still time to change.

Hermas believed there was.

The tower was still being built.
