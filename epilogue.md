## Epilogue

When I reached the end of *The Shepherd of Hermas*, I wasn't entirely sure what I thought of Hermas.

That seems like an appropriate way to finish a book I began mostly out of curiosity. I had known that *The Shepherd* was important to the early church, but I hadn't known what I would find inside it. Now I had traveled through all of its visions, commandments, towers, stones, trees, mountains, angels, virgins, and occasionally bewildering conversations. I had watched Hermas ask questions I was glad he asked, and a few I thought he probably should have figured out by then.

Somewhere along the way, though, I became fond of him.

Perhaps it was his persistence. Hermas rarely seems embarrassed by not knowing something. If he doesn't understand, he asks. If the answer doesn't make sense, he asks again. Sometimes his heavenly guides are patient with him, sometimes they tease him, and sometimes they rebuke him with a severity that made me wince. Still, Hermas keeps showing up, keeps listening, and keeps trying to become the person these visions are calling him to be.

I found that part of his story unexpectedly moving.

I come from an evangelical Christian background, and one of the things that surprised me most about *The Shepherd* was its relentless emphasis on transformation. Hermas certainly believes in God's mercy, but he doesn't seem particularly interested in allowing mercy to remain an abstract idea. Repentance means something changes. If I have been dishonest, I learn truthfulness. If desire controls me, I learn self-control. If I possess wealth while someone around me is being crushed by poverty, my responsibility isn't satisfied by feeling compassion for them. I do something.

There is something in that I want to keep.

Grace can become strangely weightless when it is separated from the life it is meant to produce. We can become so concerned with making clear that love cannot be earned that we become hesitant to say that love should transform us. Hermas has no such hesitation. Again and again, he insists that what we do matters because what we are becoming matters.

And yet there were times when I wanted to pull Hermas aside and tell him that I thought God was kinder than he seemed to imagine.

Perhaps that isn't fair to him. There is mercy throughout this book. The entire mission of the Shepherd exists because people have failed and are being given an opportunity to repent. Stones that don't fit the tower are not always abandoned. Some are set aside. Some are reshaped. Some are given time. Hermas himself is corrected again and again without being discarded.

Still, mercy in *The Shepherd* almost always arrives carrying urgency with it.

Repent while there is time. Change while there is time. Do good while there is time. The tower is being built, and eventually the building will be complete.

I find that harder to sit with.

Maybe part of my discomfort comes from living in a time when urgency has been used so often as a religious tool. Urgency can be remarkably effective at getting people to act, precisely because it doesn't leave much room for reflection. Decide now. Respond now. Don't think too long. What if this is your last opportunity? What if something happens tonight? What if the door closes before you make up your mind?

Fear has a way of narrowing the world. When we are sufficiently frightened, careful thought can begin to feel like disobedience. Questions become dangerous because there supposedly isn't time for questions. Urgency can bypass discernment and move directly toward the part of us that is afraid.

I have become wary of any spirituality that needs terror to produce transformation.

But I don't want to solve that problem by removing urgency altogether. Hermas has made that difficult for me.

There is another kind of urgency hidden within these pages, and I find myself much more receptive to it. It isn't the urgency of a frightened person trying to escape punishment. It is the urgency that comes from recognizing that a human life is actually happening now.

There are people I can love now. There are people I can help now. There are habits shaping me now, whether I am paying attention to them or not. There are things I can release, apologies I can make, kindness I can practice, wounds I can stop passing along, and ways of living that I do not need to postpone until I become some imagined future version of myself.

That kind of urgency doesn't require terror. It requires attention.

Perhaps that is why the final words of the book affected me as much as they did. After all the visions and symbols, after the enormous beast and the mysterious mountains and the elaborate explanations of stones in a tower, the final charge becomes remarkably concrete. If someone is suffering and I have the ability to help, I should help. I should not wait so long to do good that the opportunity to do it disappears.

The tower is still being built.

Of all the images in *The Shepherd of Hermas*, that may be the one that will remain with me longest. The Church is not presented as a finished monument populated by finished people. It is a construction site. Stones are arriving in all kinds of conditions. Some fit easily. Some need work. Some have cracks. Some have parts that must be cut away. Some are rejected for a time and later brought back.

There is something hopeful about imagining ourselves that way.

We are unfinished.

I suspect this is one place where Hermas and I would have a long conversation if we could somehow sit across a table from each other. He might tell me that I have become too comfortable with mercy. He might warn me that transformation is serious business, that choices have consequences, and that I should not use the goodness of God as an excuse to remain unchanged. I would want to listen carefully, because I think he would have something important to teach me.

Then I might tell him that I have learned to be suspicious of fear.

I might tell him that shame can make a person hide rather than change, and that terror can produce compliance without producing love. I might wonder aloud whether the God who patiently reshapes a human life might be even more compassionate than either of us understands. Perhaps Hermas would correct me. Perhaps I would ask another question. After spending this much time with him, that seems like the most appropriate way for the conversation to go.

I don't need to resolve that disagreement in order to be grateful for him.

That may be one of the gifts of reading an ancient Christian work that stands outside the boundaries of Scripture. I can listen without needing to defend everything I hear. I can allow Hermas to challenge my assumptions without replacing them wholesale with his own. I can recognize wisdom in his vision and limitations in it, just as I hope I can recognize both wisdom and limitation in my own.

Across nearly two thousand years, Hermas has asked me a question I wasn't expecting when I began this journey.

Not simply, *What do you believe?*

But, *What are you becoming?*

I think that is a question worth carrying with me.

I want transformation, but not transformation driven by terror. I want repentance that grows from seeing more clearly rather than hating myself for having failed. I want grace that is generous enough to receive me as I am and powerful enough not to leave me there. I want to take seriously the ways my choices shape my life and the lives around me, while trusting that the hands doing the deepest shaping are patient ones.

Perhaps that is what I have received from my time with Hermas: not a theology I can adopt without reservation, but a voice I am glad to have heard.

He has made me take transformation more seriously.

And my own journey has made me trust that I do not need to be terrified of the One transforming me.

The tower is still being built.
